Bengaluru · BBMP / Ward-level analysis

Do complaints follow the money?

BBMP publishes both halves of its own accountability loop — what residents complain about, and what it spends money on. Joining 618,202 grievances to ₹23,241 crore of work orders across 198 wards shows the two only loosely correspond. It also shows that the geography of both is a property of the denominator rather than the city: the same two columns give three opposite answers depending on what you divide them by.

Bengaluru's municipal corporation runs a grievance system that logged over 200,000 complaints in 2024 alone, and issues work orders worth roughly ₹4,650 crore a year. Both datasets are public. This analysis puts them on the same 198 wards and asks whether the places that complain most are the places that get spent on.

The short answer is: loosely, and less than the headline correlation suggests. But two things found along the way matter more than the correlation itself — where the money goes untracked, and what happens to the geography once you account for how differently sized BBMP's wards are.

FINDING 01

A third of the money belongs to no ward

Of ₹23,241 crore in gross work orders, ₹8,023 crore is booked to Untagged Expenses/Multiple Wards — spending across functional departments and non-ward projects that cannot be traced to any of the 198 wards.

Not a new finding. Citizen Matters reported this same ₹8,023 cr / ₹15,217 cr split in May 2023. It is reproduced here independently from the raw files, as a check that the pipeline reads BBMP's ledger the same way — and because it bounds everything below.

FINDING 02

Complaints and spending point at different problems

Three categories map cleanly across both datasets. Ranked by complaints they come out in almost exactly the reverse order to spending.

Streetlighting draws the most complaints by a wide margin and receives about a seventh of what roads do. For waste management — the second-largest complaint category — the ward-level correlation between complaints and spending is ρ = −0.05, statistically indistinguishable from zero.

The category mapping was checked against the grievance sub-category field rather than assumed: BBMP's Electrical category is 99.1% street and park lighting, of which 95.0% is literally Street Light Not Working.

FINDING 03

The correlation is weaker than it looks

Plotted against each other, complaints and spending trend together — but the linear fit is carried by a handful of outlier wards at the top right.

FINDING 04

The geography depends entirely on the denominator

Mapped as raw totals, both spending and complaints appear to concentrate on Bengaluru's periphery. That is largely an artefact of ward size: BBMP's outer wards are several times larger than its core wards, and distance from the centre correlates with ward area (ρ = +0.60) more strongly than with either variable of interest.

Divide by area and both maps reverse — the dense old core leads. Divide by residents instead and they reverse again, back to the periphery. All three views are computed from the same two numbers.

Neither reading is wrong. Outer wards hold more people spread over more land, so area and population pull in opposite directions, and a ward-level claim is only meaningful once it names its denominator.

This is the load-bearing finding here, and it is the one with consequences for work already published on this data — see what was already known.

FINDING 05

Spending more does not resolve more

PROVENANCE

What was already known

The May 2023 article names two limitations of its own — that it “does not correlate spending to citizen complaints or service outcomes,” and that it “can be further expanded by normalising for the area as well as population.” This project does both.

  1. Area and population normalisation. Named as future work in 2023 and not taken up since. It is the finding above, and it changes the geographic conclusion.
  2. The complaints join, systematically. 618,202 grievances against ward spending, quantified per ward across all three mappable categories over five years — rather than one category, qualitatively, in a day.
  3. Resolution rate against spending. No prior version found.
  4. A published ward-name crosswalk and a pipeline reproducible from download to figure.

A consequence for the July 2025 piece

Applying that denominator point to Decoding Bengaluru's Civic Complaints turns up a labelling error. It describes complaints as clustering in “peripheral growth areas” and calls its ward map complaint density; the map plots raw counts, and every ward it names as a hotspot is an outsized peripheral one.

Against a median ward of 1.7 km², Hemmigepura — the largest in BBMP — sits in the bottom fifth by density, and across all 198 wards the rank correlation between count and density is ρ = 0.04. The density leaders are small core wards: Chickpete, Manorayana Palya, Sri Nagar.

The conclusion survives, though, which is worth saying plainly. Per resident peripheral wards genuinely do lead, so “peripheral growth areas” holds under a denominator that piece did not use — it is the measure that does not hold, not the claim. OpenCity's own road-density piece, published 18 days later, normalises correctly.

METHOD

How the join works, and where it breaks

BBMP's grievance extracts carry a ward name and no number, spelled differently from the ward master — Bellandur against Bellanduru, Binnipet against Binni Pete. Only 51% match exactly. A crosswalk resolves them in three passes: exact match on a normalised key (136 names), fuzzy match accepted only when the best candidate clearly beats the runner-up (175), and seven hand-verified entries. 196 of 198 ward names resolve, with no collisions. The work-order file needs none of this — it carries its own ward numbers.

The per-resident reading is the one genuinely exposed assumption: it rests on Census 2011 counts applied to a periphery that has grown since. For the outer ring's complaint rate to fall to the inner ring's, outer wards would need 2.11× their 2011 population — 5.9% compounded annually for thirteen years. At a doubling it still leads, but only by 46.7 to 44.1. The per-km² and raw readings do not depend on population at all.

Known limitations

Two grievance ward names — Someshwara (9,262 complaints) and Subedarapalya (1,683) — match no ward in BBMP's 198-ward master. Someshwara turns out to exist only in BBMP's 243-ward map, as ward 3, while ward 3 in the 198-ward regime is Atturu: the extracts mix labels from two delimitation regimes. Pairing the leftovers would have merged genuinely different wards, so they are excluded — 1.7% of complaints.

The windows do not align: complaints cover 2020–24, work orders 2018–23, so this is a stock-against-stock comparison, not a causal one. Correlation is not allocation — roads cost more per unit than streetlights, and these figures show where complaints and money diverge, not that any ward was shortchanged. Rejected complaints (BBMP's Non Relevant, about 4%) are excluded from the denominator; only Closed counts as resolved.
