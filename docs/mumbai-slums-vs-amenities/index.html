Mumbai's toilets are where the slums are

Overlaying 2,541 slum clusters on ten of BMC's amenity layers. Mumbai's slums are three times closer to a public toilet than the inhabited city around them — and no closer to a park, a police station or a train. The sanitation is aimed. The shortage is not distance but how many households share the door.

Mumbai publishes the best geospatial holdings of any Indian city on OpenCity — fifteen layers, including a polygon map of every slum cluster in the city. Set those against the amenity layers and the obvious question is whether the poorest, densest settlements are the furthest from public services.

They are not. That answer turns out to be the interesting one, because the deprivation is real and the distance measure simply cannot see it.

01

Aimed at sanitation, not at services in general

A 100 metre lattice over Greater Mumbai gives — cells, — of which fall inside a slum cluster. Measured against all non-slum land, slum cells come out closer to every one of ten service types — but that baseline is not a fair one. It includes Sanjay Gandhi National Park, the mangroves, the salt pans and the airport: land with no services near it and no slums in it.

Restricting the comparison to inhabited land — non-slum cells within 300 m of a BEST stop or 400 m of a school, — of the — non-slum cells — most of the advantage disappears. What is left is specific.

What survives a fair baseline

Public toilets, at 0.31 — slum land is three times closer. Schools follow at 0.67, municipal schools at 0.80. Health centres and funeral sites sit at parity. Parks, police stations, fire stations, suburban stations and bus stops all come out slightly further, between 1.03 and 1.29.

The toilet result is the one that does not move. Across three independently-defined baselines it ranges only from 0.19 to 0.35, and it is the sole service that is never near parity. Within each ward — where both groups share the same surrounding geography — slum land is closer to a public toilet in 21 of 21 wards, to a school in 19, to a health centre in 17.

This is what a targeted programme looks like in data. BMC builds community toilets in slums, because that is where households have no latrine of their own. It has not similarly aimed its parks or its fire stations, and the same measurement shows that too.

02

Half the city's toilet seats stand on seven per cent of its land

Slum clusters cover — km² — — of Greater Mumbai. Inside that footprint sit — toilet seats, — of every seat in the city.

That concentration is the mechanism behind finding 01, and it is the strongest evidence here that BMC's toilet programme is aimed where it should be — it needs no baseline and no denominator at all. A third of clusters have a toilet block inside their own boundary; the median cluster is 23 metres from one. No other service is remotely as concentrated: 7.4% of clusters contain a school, 1.0% a health centre, none a fire station.

03

The deficit is load, not location

Proximity says nothing about how many people are in the queue. The census supplies the missing denominator: table HH-14 of the 2011 Houselisting Census records, for every ward, the share of households with no latrine inside the premises that depend on a public one.

The number that matters

— of Greater Mumbai's households — — of them — depended on a public latrine. Against — seats on BMC's own layer, that is — households sharing every seat, or at least 63 people per seat — a lower bound, since it applies a household share to a population count and latrine-dependent households are, if anything, larger than average.

Distance is measured in tens of metres. Load is measured in dozens of households. Only one of those is a shortage.

Ward E — Byculla and Mazgaon — carries 29 households per seat, three times ward T's 10. E is not a ward with much slum land (3.4%); it is an old, dense, chawl-and-tenement ward where a quarter of households still share a public latrine and only 637 seats serve them.

On the statistics. Mumbai has 24 wards. At that n a Spearman coefficient needs about 0.41 to clear the 5% level, so no ward-level correlation is reported here as a finding. The inference lives at cluster level (n = 2,541) and grid-cell level (n = 47,450); the ward tables describe 24 named units and nothing more.

04

BMC's map and BMC's RTI replies disagree about BMC's toilets

Praja Foundation obtained ward-wise toilet figures from BMC under the Right to Information Act and reported, in May 2024, that only one public toilet seat in four is for women — a 6:1 male-to-female ratio in C ward, 4:1 in B, 3:1 in A.

The published GIS layer, from the same authority, records — of seats as female. Its worst ward is also C, at 2.2:1, followed by B — the same ordering, less than half the magnitude.

Unresolved

These figures cannot both describe the same toilets, and the obvious explanation does not hold. If the map layer pooled public toilets with community toilets in slums, it should contain a male-skewed subset corresponding to Praja's 25%. It does not: seats inside slum clusters are 49.2% female and seats outside them 48.0% — the layer is uniformly near-parity everywhere, with no sub-population that could be the RTI figure.

So the ranking agrees, the level does not, and a public-versus-community split within this layer does not account for the gap. Neither source states its scope on its face. Anyone using either number for policy needs that resolved first, and it is not resolvable from the published data.

05

What was already known

The sanitation side of this has been reported, and this analysis is positioned against that work rather than around it.

Status of Civic Issues in Mumbai — Praja Foundation, May 2024
The definitive prior work. One seat per 752 men and 1,820 women; community toilet seats adequate for a third of the slum population; 69% of blocks without a water connection. Sourced by RTI, reported per ward, not spatial. It states plainly: "Data on ward wise slum population is not available."
Delhi Slums: a comparative look at public facilities — OpenCity
The nearest methodological precedent on the platform, for a different city. No Mumbai equivalent has been published.

What this adds:

  1. The spatial join itself. Slum polygons against ten amenity layers, per cluster and per 100 m cell — a measurement Praja's ward-level RTI tables cannot make and OpenCity has not published for Mumbai.
  2. A measured denominator where Praja records a gap. Ward-wise slum population is genuinely unpublished. Rather than estimate it, this uses two things that are measured: slum area from the cluster map, and households dependent on a public latrine from Census HH-14. Both are stated, neither is modelled.
  3. The proximity result, with its control. That slum land is closer to services — and that this survives a within-ward comparison — reframes the question from siting to capacity.
  4. A discrepancy between two BMC sources on the female seat share, which is visible only by putting the map layer and the RTI figures side by side.
06

How this was built, and where it is weak

Wards are assigned by spatial join, never by the ward field the layers carry — geometry is what is being analysed. The two were compared anyway, and the disagreement rate is itself a data-quality finding: 3.1% on the toilet layer, 13.9% on health UPHCs. Per-ward counts rank almost identically either way (ρ ≥ 0.81), so the tables here do not depend on the choice.
